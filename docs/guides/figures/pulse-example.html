How one pulse becomes a bound

Start to finish for a single calibration pulse: what the samples actually say, why the answer is a region rather than a point, what “fits well enough” actually means, how the block search maps the region without gaps, and what the 165,000-evaluation boundary is protecting.

The capture below is real in every capture-level number (sampler cadence, pulse protocol, budget, corpus demands). The per-rectangle geometry is an honest illustration of the algorithm — the detector does not retain its internal rectangle log, so the shapes are drawn to teach, not replayed from disk.

1 · What the samples actually say

Take pulse #23 of the 59 in a calibration capture. The instrument commanded the graphics processor to run a matrix-multiply load for exactly 1.000 s, starting at a known wall-clock time. The power counter doesn't see edges — it reports one average per interval, roughly every 112 ms. Samples wholly inside the pulse read the plateau; samples wholly outside read the baseline; and the one or two samples that straddle each edge read something in between, in proportion to how much of their interval the pulse covered.

2 · Two unknowns make a plane of guesses

The pulse has exactly two unknowns: how far the start edge really was from its commanded time, and how far the stop edge really was from its commanded time. A complete guess about the pulse is therefore a pair of numbers. “Started 3.2 ms late, stopped 1.1 ms early” is one guess. “Started 40 ms late, stopped 40 ms late” is another.

Put start-shift on a horizontal axis and stop-shift on a vertical axis and every possible guess becomes one point on a plane — the same way every possible (voltage, current) operating point of a device is one point on an I-V plane. That is the entire meaning of “the plane” here: each point is one hypothesis about where the two edges really were.

Any guess can be tested against Figure 1's samples. Take the (+40 ms, +40 ms) guess: if the whole pulse really sat 40 ms late, the sample straddling the start edge would have overlapped the pulse for 40 ms less, so its measured average should sit visibly lower than it actually does — the prediction misses the recorded height by far more than sample noise can explain, and the guess is ruled out. Now take (+0.3 ms, −0.2 ms): shifting an edge by a fraction of a millisecond changes the straddling sample's predicted height by a fraction of a percent — less than ordinary noise. The samples cannot tell that guess apart from the perfect one.

That second case is the whole problem. It is not one lucky guess that survives; it is an entire connected patch of the plane — every guess close enough that the straddling samples can't testify against it. Keeping only the single best-scoring guess would pretend the samples pinpoint the edges. They don't. The honest answer is the whole surviving patch, and the pulse's timing bound is the worst edge displacement anywhere in it. Which creates the obligation that drives everything in the next section: the patch must be found without gaps, because a missed corner of it might contain the guess that should have set the bound.

3 · What “fits well enough” means — the score and the waterline

Sections 1–2 said guesses get “ruled out” or “survive.” Here is the actual test, so those words carry no hidden weight. Every guess gets a score: for each sample, take the miss — measured height minus the height this guess predicts — divide it by the sample noise so the units are “how many noise-widths off,” pass it through a penalty that grows with the square of small misses but only linearly for big ones (so one glitchy sample can't dominate the verdict), and add the penalties up. A perfect explanation scores near zero; a guess whose straddle-sample predictions are badly wrong piles up penalty fast.

Two gates use that score. The first asks whether there is a pulse at all: the best guess's score must be less than half the score of the flat “no pulse happened” explanation, or the pulse is declared not detected and the capture is refused. The second gate defines the surviving patch. Its threshold is not an absolute number — it is set relative to the best guess:

limit = best score + max( 1.0, 5% of best score )

In words: a guess survives if it explains the samples essentially as well as the best one — within about one sample's worth of ordinary noise, or within 5% of the total score when the total is large. That “1.0” floor is what makes the patch honest: even if the best fit is nearly perfect, any guess whose penalty is within a single noise-width of it cannot be told apart from it by this data, so it stays in.

One subtlety, stated so nobody discovers it later as a gotcha: the waterline depends on the best score, so the detector first finds the best fit, then floods the valley around it. And after the patch is mapped, it is widened once more by the recorded uncertainty of the pulse's own command timestamps — the map bounds what the samples can say, and the command stamps carry their own small slop.

4 · Testing guesses in blocks — what the rectangles are

The surviving patch has to be found, and there are infinitely many guesses to test. Testing them one at a time is impossible, and testing a finite grid of them is worse than it looks: whatever spacing you choose, the guesses between your grid points go untested, and the patch's true extent could poke out between them.

So the detector tests guesses in blocks. A rectangle is nothing mysterious — it is a range of start-shifts crossed with a range of stop-shifts: “every guess where the start was off by between −500 and −250 ms and the stop was off by between +250 and +500 ms” is one rectangle, containing infinitely many guesses. The reason blocks work is a property of the fit itself: because sliding an edge changes a straddling sample's predicted height smoothly and in one direction, the detector can compute — exactly, not by sampling — the best score any guess inside the block could possibly achieve. That one computation is one “evaluation,” and it licenses one of three moves:

Reject the block — if even its best possible score sits above the waterline of Figure 3, every guess inside is ruled out in one stroke. This is the move that makes the search finite: one evaluation can eliminate a quarter of the plane. Split the block — if it can't be rejected (some guess inside might fit), cut it in half and evaluate each half. Blocks that straddle the patch's edge keep splitting, homing in on the boundary. Accept the block — once a surviving block is only 0.1 ms on each side, stop splitting and count its entire extent into the patch — the conservative move, since nothing inside it could be ruled out.

A typical healthy pulse finishes this in a couple of thousand evaluations — the whole 59-pulse capture lands between 115,449 and 137,535, roughly 2,000–2,300 per pulse. The capture's b_fiducial is then the worst edge displacement across all 59 completed maps, plus the capture's clock-anchor bound.

5 · What the 165,000 boundary is

The budget is a cap on the total number of rectangle evaluations across all 59 maps. It is not a quality threshold — it is two protections in one number. It makes the verdict reproducible: the same trace always costs the same evaluations and gets the same verdict on any machine, rather than depending on machine speed or operator patience. And it makes effort itself a diagnosis: crisp steps die cheaply, so a trace that needs far more work than any healthy capture ever has is a trace whose edges are physically smeared — something on the machine blurred what the map is trying to trace.

Why refusal is total, never partial. If the budget runs out mid-map, the unexplored territory might contain acceptable candidates — including the one that should have set the bound. A bound computed from an unfinished map could only err in the dangerous direction: too small. So the capture is refused as non-convergent, and the refusal is recorded, not discarded.

That is the whole pipeline for one pulse: samples → a scored plane of candidates → a gapless rectangle map of what survives → the worst survivor as the bound → the effort count checked against a boundary calibrated from every healthy capture the machine has ever produced. Multiply by 59, take the worst, add the clock-anchor term, and you have the capture's b_fiducial — the number the bracket screen, the drift ceiling, and every phase-energy interval downstream inherit.
